<div class="case-block">
  <div class="case-block-label">🤔 The problem</div>
  <p>Classrooms are increasingly mixed in terms of what languages children speak at home, and teachers cannot realistically give every child support in their own first language. A robot that can be programmed to speak more than one language looked like a promising way to solve this. When a robot teaches a child a new word in a second language, should it also give the translation in the child's first language, or should it stick to the new language only?</p>
</div>

<div class="case-block">
  <div class="case-block-label">🎯 Why this mattered</div>
  <p>This project was carried out within <a href="http://l2tor.eu" target="_blank" rel="noopener">L2TOR</a>, funded by the European Union's Horizon 2020 research and innovation programme. It focused on a group past research has flagged as vulnerable: Turkish-Dutch kindergartners, whose Dutch language skills and school outcomes tend to lag behind their monolingual Dutch peers. The aim was not to replace teachers, but to test whether a robot could work alongside them, offering one-on-one language support in a classroom where no teacher can realistically speak every child's first language.</p>
</div>

<div class="case-block">
  <div class="case-block-label">🤝 My role</div>
  <p>This project spanned four universities in the Netherlands. I took part in the whole process: designing the experiment, preparing the gamified lesson materials, building the lesson into the robot, running early tests, and analyzing the data.</p>
</div>

<div class="case-block">
  <div class="case-block-label">🎮 The task</div>
  <p>Sixty-seven Turkish-Dutch kindergartners, all of whom already knew certain words in Turkish but not yet in Dutch, were taught six new Dutch words by a NAO robot through a tablet game.</p>
  <div class="vs-compare">
    <span class="vs-chip">Dutch-only robot</span>
    <span class="vs-versus">vs</span>
    <span class="vs-chip">Dutch + Turkish translation</span>
  </div>
  <p>Every child got both versions, so each child acted as their own comparison. Word knowledge was tested right after the session, and again about a week later.</p>
</div>

<div class="case-result">
  <span class="case-result-label">Result</span>
  Children learned more new words from the Dutch-only robot than from the robot that gave Turkish translations — the opposite of what the team expected going in. This held up both right after the lesson and a week later. Children who already knew more words, in either language, learned more overall, regardless of which robot taught them.
</div>

<div class="case-insight">
  <div class="case-insight-label">💡 What this means in practice</div>
  <p>Most children said they liked the bilingual robot better, even though they learned less from it. What people prefer and what actually helps them is not always the same thing, and testing the two separately, rather than assuming a well-liked feature is also an effective one, is the only way to catch that gap.</p>
</div>
